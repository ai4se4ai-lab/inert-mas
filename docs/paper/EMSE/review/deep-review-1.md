# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper addresses the security of Large Language Model (LLM) multi-agent systems (MAS) against prompt injection propagation. Grounded in a systematic mapping study of 543 papers, the author introduces a taxonomy of seven injection channels classified by the receiving handoff interface and the protected decision it authorizes. Building on this taxonomy, the paper presents INERT, a model-driven security framework that compiles a developer-authored typed workflow model into a content-hashed execution plan enforced at runtime by an LLM-free deterministic reference monitor. The controller mediates context selection, proposal acceptance, tool execution, and snapshot release via per-request HMAC capabilities, dependency-key freshness (incorporating integrity labels), pinned tool metadata hashes, and lattice-based taint non-interference. Furthermore, the same workflow model drives an automated model-based security test generator across six channel-derived coverage criteria (`EDGE`, `SLOT`, `SEL`, `TAINT`, `RECONF`, `COLL`) paired with a tamper-evident hash-chained replay auditor. Extensive empirical evaluation across 106,382 runs on a 51-workflow topology zoo and the TAMAS benchmark with six open-weight models demonstrates that INERT reduces the attack success rate on the topology zoo from 82.4% to 0.0% while achieving 82.2% benign task utility, outperforming message-level detectors, single-agent flow-control planners, and LLM-generated control-flow graphs.

## Strengths
* **Actionable Channel Taxonomy and Architectural Separation of Controls:** By classifying multi-agent vulnerabilities by the receiving handoff interface and protected decision rather than payload phrasing or emergent failure modes, the taxonomy cleanly separates *structural* attacks (eliminated by construction), *semantic* data-to-executor flows (mediated by taint policy), and *residual* authority risks, directly mapping each leaf to a formal reference-monitor check and test coverage obligation.
* **Unified Model-Driven Reference Monitor with Embedded Provenance:** Compiling a single typed workflow model into an LLM-free controller that embeds lattice integrity labels directly inside cryptographic dependency keys and enforces per-request HMAC capabilities, pinned tool hashes, and dual taint checks (at both context selection and proposal acceptance) provides strong deterministic security guarantees without degrading benign utility to the level of single-agent quarantine planners.
* **Systematic Model-Based Security Testing and LLM-Free Replay Auditing:** Deriving executable prompt-injection test obligations automatically from the compiled workflow model across six coverage criteria—paired with sandbox side-effect oracles and a hash-chained cryptographic replay auditor—eliminates LLM-as-a-judge unreliability and uncovers nearly five times as many distinct violated workflow obligations at equal cost compared to static single-entry benchmark suites.
* **Comprehensive Empirical Design and Ablation Transparency:** The evaluation spans 51 workflows across five topologies, six open-weight models, an adaptive attacker, and systematic ablations of every individual security predicate, clearly demonstrating that removing each mechanism reopens the exact attack channel predicted by the taxonomy.

## Weaknesses
* **Implicit-Flow Preconditions in Tool Tainting and Unexercised Declassifiers:** Please consider clarifying the soundness preconditions of Proposition 1 and evaluating explicit declassifier slots, as omitting `data`-role arguments from tool output labels ($\ell_{out}$) could allow untrusted query parameters or value-echoing helper tools to influence downstream outputs without taint propagation, and leaving all `L3` paths unresolved without `declassify` slots across the topology zoo sacrifices benign utility on `reply` tasks while leaving declassifier robustness untested against the adaptive attacker.
* **Baseline Simplifications, Missing Multi-Agent Defenses, and Orchestration Confounding:** The comparative security and utility claims would be strengthened by addressing the simplifications in baselines B3 (replacing CaMeL's restricted Python interpreter with static JSON plans, which depresses its benign utility to 44.0%) and B4 (omitting ControlValve's zero-shot per-edge contextual rules, inflating its false-rejection rate to 54.0%), isolating the scaffolding gains of structured single-question slots (I1 vs. B0) from the cost of security mediation, and either empirically comparing against or justifying the omission of multi-agent information-flow and graph-pruning defenses cited in the paper (Reference Paper: Defeating Prompt Injections by Design, Debenedetti et al., 2025; Reference Paper: Breaking and Fixing Defenses Against Control-Flow Hijacking in Multi-Agent Systems, Jha et al., 2025; Reference Paper: AgentSafe: Safeguarding Large Language Model-based Multi-agent Systems via Hierarchical Data Management, Mao et al., 2025; Reference Paper: G-Safeguard: A Topology-Guided Security Lens and Treatment on LLM-based Multi-agent Systems, Wang et al., 2025; Reference Paper: BlindGuard: Safeguarding LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems under Unknown Attacks, Miao et al., 2025).
* **TAMAS Adaptation Dynamics, Residual Risk Double-Counting, and Additional Multi-Agent Benchmarks:** Adapting TAMAS into a fixed round-robin slot schedule with custom IPI payloads and a harmful-tool heuristic alters its emergent conversation routing, and summing `DPI-w` and `DPI-s` into the 390 residual failures double-counts the same direct-injection tasks while conflating 45 benign-tool heuristic overlaps with actual taint breaches; the authors might also consider discussing or evaluating standardized multi-agent cascading benchmarks (Reference Paper: TAMAS: Benchmarking Adversarial Risks in Multi-Agent LLM Systems, Kavathekar et al., 2025; Reference Paper: ACIArena: Toward Unified Evaluation for Agent Cascading Injection, An et al., 2026).
* **Single-Rater Corpus Coding and Search Reproducibility in the Mapping Study:** Because the 543-study systematic mapping corpus relies on an unnamed primary search engine, a 5-seed snowballing citation threshold, 164 title-only Scopus screenings, and single-author LLM-assisted coding (where a keyword classifier achieved 40% agreement), please consider naming the primary database, reporting inter-rater reliability on a sample with an independent human rater, and refining the related-work characterizations of recent interface-based taxonomies and structural workflow testing (Reference Paper: SoK: When Safe Agents Fail Together: The Security of Multi Agent LLM Systems, Yang et al., 2026; Reference Paper: Testing Agentic Workflows with Structural Coverage Criteria, Kahani & Bagherzadeh, 2026; Reference Paper: Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?, Cemri et al., 2025).
* **Internal Traceability and Experimental Accounting Gaps:** The manuscript would benefit from harmonizing internal details across sections, including reconciling the cross-session scope exclusion of Channel C6 (Gap G5) with the full-coverage marks for R5 and R8 in Table 3, clarifying the agent wrapper TCB boundary, explaining the missing `ValidatorReject` reason codes and `A4` ablation interactions in Figure 8, and providing a complete arithmetic breakdown of the 51 workflows and 106,382 total runs.

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Background Foundations & Running Example] (Pages: 1-7)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Dual Lattice Polarity in Integrity and Declassification Definitions (Section 2.4, Definitions 7 and 8, Page 5):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Definition 7, the partial order $\sqsubseteq$ on integrity labels is defined verbally as "at least as trustworthy as," and the prose states that a policy assigns each sink the "highest label it may receive" and that non-interference holds when "values labelled above its level cannot influence what reaches it." In Definition 8, a declassifier is defined as assigning an input a "lower label (*endorsement* in integrity terminology)." The concrete chain order is not introduced until Section 5.1 (page 16), where $\mathcal{L} = \mathit{trusted} \sqsubset \mathit{user} \sqsubset \mathit{agent\_untrusted} \sqsubset \mathit{external\_untrusted}$ with join $\sqcup$. While the mathematical formulation in Section 5 is internally consistent (using a taint/distrust ordering where $\sqsubset$ means "strictly less tainted / more trustworthy than" and $\sqcup$ computes the least trustworthy contributing source), combining this inverted ordering with classical Biba/Denning integrity terminology in Definitions 7 and 8 is a potential concern for clarity. In classical integrity lattices (e.g., Biba [32]), *higher* integrity corresponds to *greater* trustworthiness ($\mathit{untrusted} \sqsubset \mathit{trusted}$), and endorsement *raises* an integrity label rather than lowering it. Because the paper simultaneously refers to $\mathcal{H}$ as "high-integrity roles" and "high-integrity sinks" (pages 7 and 16) while placing $\mathit{trusted}$ at the bottom of $\sqsubseteq$, terms such as "highest label," "above its level," and "lower label (endorsement)" in Section 2.4 can be misread before the reader reaches Section 5.1.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly state in Definition 7 that the lattice is ordered by increasing taint (or decreasing trustworthiness), with $\mathit{trusted}$ as the bottom element ($\bot$) and $\mathit{external\_untrusted}$ as the top element ($\top$), so that higher elements in $\sqsubseteq$ represent less trustworthy sources, $\sqcup$ computes the least trustworthy bound, and endorsement moves a label downward toward $\mathit{trusted}$.

2. **Framing of the Multi-Agent Mediation Gap Relative to Existing MAS Monitors (Section 1, Page 2):**
   - **Potential Concern:** When motivating the mediation gap in the second paragraph of Section 1, the introduction contrasts INERT solely against message-level defenses that "judge each message in isolation" [11–14], single-agent flow control (CaMeL [15]), and LLM-generated control-flow graphs (ControlValve [16]). Because existing multi-agent data-management and topology-monitoring defenses (such as AgentSafe [53], BlindGuard [56], and G-Safeguard [57], which are discussed later in Table 3 and Section 10.3) specifically target multi-agent propagation rather than single messages or single agents, omitting them in the opening gap analysis is a potential concern for framing completeness.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Briefly acknowledge multi-agent data-management and topology-monitoring defenses [53, 56, 57] when defining the mediation gap in Section 1, explaining why their reliance on heuristic/learned monitors or lack of request-bound authorship, freshness, and designer-compiled reference models still leaves the mediation gap open.

3. **Scope of the 106,382 Run Count in the Abstract (Abstract, Page 1):**
   - **Potential Concern:** The Abstract states: "Over 106,382 runs, INERT lowered the attack success rate from 82.4% without defense to 0.0%, matching the strongest baseline, a CaMeL-style planner with quarantined data, while completing 82.2% of benign tasks against that baseline's 44.0%." As clarified in Section 1 (pages 2–3) and Section 7 (Table 6, page 24), 106,382 is the total number of runs across the entire study (combining the topology zoo, benign runs, ablations, adaptive attacker, and TAMAS), whereas the 82.4% to 0.0% ASR reduction and 82.2% vs. 44.0% utility figures apply specifically to the 51-workflow topology zoo (with TAMAS reported in the subsequent sentence).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Adjust the phrasing in the Abstract to mirror the precision of Section 1—for example: "In a study of 106,382 total runs, INERT lowered the attack success rate on the 51-workflow topology zoo from 82.4% without defense to 0.0%..."

### [The INERT Architecture: Reference Monitor, Taint Policy, Replay Audit & Test Generation] (Pages: 15-21)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Exclusion of `data`-Role Arguments from Tool Output Labels ($\lambda(s)$ and $\ell_{out}$) and Its Impact on Taint Non-Interference (Proposition 1)**
   - **Context:** In Section 5.2 (page 17), the static label inference rule for a tool slot ($k(s) = \text{tool}$) is defined as:
     $$\lambda(s) = \ell_{t(s)} \sqcup \bigsqcup \{\lambda(src(a)) \mid \rho(a) \neq \texttt{data}\}$$
     Similarly, at runtime in Section 5.3 (Mediation point 3, page 18), a tool slot's output receives the label:
     $$\ell_{out} = \ell_{env} \sqcup \ell_t \sqcup \bigsqcup_{\rho(a) \neq \texttt{data}} \ell_{src(a)}.$$
     The text justifies omitting `data` arguments by stating: *"Data arguments do not taint a tool's output because they cannot change what the tool does."*
   - **Potential Concern:** Proposition 1 (page 19) states that in any run with all mechanisms enabled, *every value that could have influenced* an accepted value in a field $f$ with $\rho(f) \in \mathcal{H}$, or an argument $a$ with $\rho(a) \in \mathcal{H}$ of an executed tool, has a label at most $\mu(\rho)$, unless it passed through a `declassify` slot. While a `data` argument does not alter *which* operation or side effect a deterministic write-only sink tool performs (such as `body` in `send_email`, where the tool merely returns a delivery acknowledgment), it can directly determine or be echoed inside the *value* returned by a tool if a workflow includes a deterministic helper, parsing, formatting, or state-echoing tool whose return value depends on its `data` argument. If such a tool has base and environment labels $\ell_{env} \sqcup \ell_t \sqsubseteq \mathit{agent\_untrusted}$ and receives an $\mathit{external\_untrusted}$ payload via a `data` argument, excluding $\rho(a) = \texttt{data}$ from $\ell_{out}$ and $\lambda(s)$ assigns the tool's output a lower (more trustworthy) label without passing through a `declassify` slot, bypassing linter check L3 and violating Proposition 1.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Explicitly state as a domain/linter constraint in Section 5.2 that tools with `data` arguments are restricted to write-only side-effect parameters whose return values are functionally independent of those arguments (i.e., any argument that can influence a tool's return value read by downstream slots must not be typed as $\texttt{data}$), or include $\ell_{src(a)}$ in $\ell_{out}$ and $\lambda(s)$ whenever a tool's output depends on $a$ and is read downstream.

2. **Discrepancies Between Static Label Inference ($\lambda(s)$) and Runtime Taint Propagation ($\ell_{out}$, Declassifiers, and Context Withholding)**
   - **Context:** In Section 5.2 (page 17), the linter infers a static label $\lambda(s)$ that *"bounds the label each slot's value can carry,"* defined piecewise over $k(s) \in \{\text{input}, \text{agent}, \text{tool}, \text{declassify}\}$. Under the integrity lattice $\mathcal{L} = \mathit{trusted} \sqsubset \mathit{user} \sqsubset \mathit{agent\_untrusted} \sqsubset \mathit{external\_untrusted}$ (page 16), less trustworthy labels sit higher in the lattice.
   - **Potential Concern:** Comparing the static formula $\lambda(s)$ against the runtime rules in Section 5.3 (page 18) reveals three discrepancies:
     1. *Environment resource labels in tool slots:* At runtime, $\ell_{out}$ joins the environment resource label $\ell_{env}$ (e.g., $\mathit{external\_untrusted}$ for an outside e-mail or $\mathit{user}$ for a workspace file), whereas $\ell_{env}$ does not appear in the static formula $\lambda(s) = \ell_{t(s)} \sqcup \bigsqcup \{\lambda(src(a)) \mid \rho(a) \neq \texttt{data}\}$. Unless $\ell_{t(s)}$ in the static rule is explicitly defined to incorporate the worst-case environment resource label $\ell_{env}$ reachable by tool $t(s)$, $\lambda(s)$ is not guaranteed to upper-bound $\ell_{out}$. For instance, in the running example (`chain3_email`, page 17), `fetch` (`read_email`) only takes `intent.index` ($\lambda(\texttt{intent}) = \mathit{agent\_untrusted}$), yet $\lambda(\texttt{fetch})$ evaluates to $\mathit{external\_untrusted}$, which requires incorporating the e-mail resource label.
     2. *Declassifier failure paths and implicit flows:* For $k(s) = \text{declassify}$, the static rule sets $\lambda(s) = \ell_{D(s)}$. However, at runtime (page 18), a declassifier *"commits its extracted value at $\ell_D$ on success and at the source label on failure."* If extraction fails at runtime, the slot commits at $\ell_{src} \sqsupset \ell_{D(s)}$, exceeding $\lambda(s)$. Moreover, because an attacker who controls an $\mathit{external\_untrusted}$ input can control whether pattern extraction succeeds or fails (cf. Definition 8 on page 5, where a declassifier is robust when the attacker cannot control whether the lowering happens), a downstream slot $s$ with threshold $m_s = \ell_D$ will have the declassifier read $r$ included in $C_q$ on success and withheld on failure. Because MP1 states that *"Withheld reads are named but not shown"* (page 18), toggling whether $r$ is shown or named as withheld in the prompt of $s$ creates a 1-bit implicit information flow from untrusted data into the prompt of $s$ that is not accounted for in the proof sketch of Proposition 1 (page 19).
     3. *Pre-filtering vs. post-filtering agent labels and multi-field slots:* For $k(s) = \text{agent}$, $\lambda(s) = \ell_{\mathcal{A}} \sqcup \bigsqcup_{u \in pre(s)} \lambda(u)$ joins over all declared prerequisites $pre(s)$ before MP1 context selection, whereas at runtime MP1 withholds reads with $\ell_r \not\sqsubseteq m_s$ so that $\ell_q \sqsubseteq m_s$ (e.g., on page 17, $\lambda(\texttt{act}) = \mathit{external\_untrusted}$, whereas at runtime `a1` is withheld and `act` commits at $\mathit{agent\_untrusted}$). In multi-hop workflows where a designer relies on runtime withholding rather than inserting a declassifier, $\lambda(s)$ over-taints $s$ and causes cascading false-positive L3 warnings on downstream slots. Similarly, because a single scalar label $\ell_q$ and threshold $m_s = \min_{\sqsubseteq}\{\mu(\rho(f)) \mid f \in F(s), \rho(f) \in \mathcal{H}\}$ apply to an entire slot, an agent slot declaring both a `data` field and a high-integrity field will have all `external_untrusted` reads withheld by MP1, starving the `data` field.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section 5.2 that $\ell_{t(s)}$ incorporates the worst-case $\ell_{env}$ for tool $t(s)$, that $\lambda(s)$ is computed prior to MP1 withholding to surface all unmediated paths (assuming single-role or separated slots), and either have failed declassifications fail closed via $\textsc{Fail}(q)$ or state that $\lambda(s)$ and Proposition 1 cover explicit data flows under successful extraction.

3. **Underspecification of `declassify` Slots, Control-Field Guards, and Audit Logging in Algorithms 1 and 2**
   - **Context:** Section 5.2 (page 16) defines four slot kinds $k(s) \in \{\text{input}, \text{agent}, \text{tool}, \text{declassify}\}$ and states that *"A slot may carry a guard on a control field"* (with linter check L5 on page 17 flagging free-text or data fields wired into guards). #### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Confounding of Runtime Context Withholding and Declassification Omission on the `reply` Task Family, and Its Impact on RQ4 and Hypothesis H5 (Sections 6.1, 6.2, 7.4, and 7.5, pp. 21–22, 27–29)**
   - **Classification:** Potential concern regarding task design, mechanism evaluation, and utility interpretation.
   - **Discussion:** Earlier sections motivate `declassify` slots specifically for workflows that legitimately need an outside fact to determine an execution parameter, stating: *"A policy that never lowers labels is useless when the executor needs an outside fact, such as the sender to reply to"* (Section 2.4, p. 5; Section 5.2, p. 16). In Section 6.2 (p. 22), the `reply` task family is defined such that *"the summary goes back to the sender, so the recipient must come from untrusted content."* Immediately below, however, the text states that across all 51 topology-zoo models, *"every middle agent reads its upstream agents and the tool output, and the executor reads the final analysis and the request, so the linter flags L3 paths in every model and enforcement is left to the runtime."* Under Mediation Point 1 (`MP1`, Section 5.3, p. 18), leaving static `L3` warnings unresolved without a `declassify` slot causes the runtime controller to withhold any read whose label exceeds the sink threshold ($m_s = \mathit{agent\_untrusted}$ for executor fields) from the executor's selected context $C_q$. Furthermore, executor slots carry validators requiring the recipient to be grounded in the executor's inputs (Section 5.2, p. 17). Consequently, in `reply` tasks where the sender's address resides in the fetched untrusted e-mail or upstream analysis rather than the user's `task` request, withholding the untrusted read under `I2` makes it structurally impossible for the executor to see the legitimate recipient address on benign runs. This design choice has three important implications that merit clarification:
     - *Benign utility drop (`I1` vs. `I2`):* In Table 6 (p. 24) and Section 7.4 (p. 27), benign utility drops by 9.7 percentage points from `I1` ($91.9\%$, where taint withholding is disabled) to `I2` ($82.2\%$), with a bimodal distribution for `I2` in Figure 6b, which the prose notes is *"concentrated in tasks whose executor needs information from untrusted material."* It is unclear whether any of the 51 topology-zoo workflows actually instantiate `declassify` slots, or if `reply` models intentionally omitted declassifiers to measure the raw utility penalty of strict taint withholding. Reporting benign utility broken down by task family (`email`, `reply`, `web`, `code`)—and evaluating `reply` with a bounded `declassify` slot—would separate the utility of INERT's endorsement mechanism from the penalty of omitting required declassifiers.
     - *Diagnostic comparison with `B3`:* Section 7 (p. 23) attributes `I2`'s benign utility advantage over the `CaMeL`-style baseline `B3` ($82.2\%$ vs. $44.0\%$) to the fact that *"B3's planner never sees the material it must act on and its capability policy denies side effects whose arguments derive from it."* Yet without declassifiers, `I2` enforces the same restriction on `executor` arguments derived from untrusted reads. Breaking down utility by task family would clarify whether both `I2` and `B3` fail on `reply` due to omitted declassifiers while `B3` additionally fails on other task families (e.g., if `B3`'s capability policy also blocks untrusted content in `data` sinks like `do.body` or when tool selection depends on intermediate outputs).
     - *Evaluation of Hypothesis H5 (RQ5):* Pre-registered Hypothesis H5 (Section 6.1, p. 21) predicts that under a 10-attempt adaptive attacker (`A6`), *"residual successes concentrate at declassifiers and slots with legitimate authority."* Because the 36 adaptive-attacker cases on the topology zoo (Section 7.5, p. 29) omit `declassify` slots and rely on `MP1` context withholding (yielding $0.0\%$ adaptive success because no payload text inside an untrusted source can alter its static/dynamic provenance label $\ell_{\text{env}} = \mathit{external\_untrusted}$ before `MP1` withholds it from the executor), `A6` never tests whether an adaptive adversary can fool a bounded regex or enumeration declassifier within its declared extraction bound.

2. **Conflation of Structured Slot Orchestration Gains with Security Policy Cost in Benign Utility and Efficiency Comparisons (Sections 6.1, 7, 7.4, Table 6, and Figure 10, pp. 21, 23–24, 27–29)**
   - **Classification:** Potential concern regarding internal validity and baseline parity in RQ4.
   - **Discussion:** For RQ4, Hypothesis H4 (Section 6.1, p. 21) evaluates INERT's defense cost relative to the undefended baseline `B0`. In Table 6 (p. 24) and Section 7.4 (pp. 27–29), full INERT (`I2`) achieves higher benign utility ($82.2\%$) and lower token/latency cost ($2,946$ mean / $2.4\text{k}$ median tokens, $8.2$ calls, $56\text{s}$ median) than undefended `B0` ($71.4\%$ utility, $5,424$ mean / $4.1\text{k}$ median tokens, $10.0$ calls, $80\text{s}$ median), leading to the conclusion on p. 29 that *"INERT's utility cost is negative relative to the undefended system on our tasks."* However, transitioning from `B0` to `I2` simultaneously changes two orthogonal factors:
     1. *The coordination and prompting substrate:* replacing free-form group-chat turn-taking over a growing shared transcript (`AutoGen`/Swarm semantics in `B0`–`B2`) with a deterministic data-flow schedule of single-question typed slots and JSON-schema constrained decoding (`I1` and `I2`).
     2. *The security reference monitor:* enforcing capability tokens, key freshness, validators, and taint non-interference.

Clarifying how secondary checks (`Contract`, `Flow`, or `Exec`) catch part of the `TokenInvalid` cases when tokens are disabled, and why `I2 - key match` reaches $73\%$ on `A5`, would make Figure 8a and Figure 8b fully consistent.
     - *Subset vs. full-grid utility under attack ($U_{att}$):* Section 7.2 (p. 25) states that *"utility under attack is 0.0% without selection against 56.4% for I2,"* whereas Table 6 (p. 24) and Section 7.4 (p. 29) report $U_{att} = 61.4\%$ for `I2`. Explicitly noting in Section 7.2 that $56.4\%$ is the $U_{att}$ on the 17-workflow, 4-model ablation subset ($1,700$ runs per condition) rather than the 51-workflow, 6-model full grid ($8,670$ runs), and noting that removing `taint@select` while keeping `taint@accept` also causes *benign* runs with `L3` paths to fail $100\%$ of the time at acceptance, would prevent confusion.

4. **Double-Counting of `DPI-w` and `DPI-s` Configurations and Heuristic Oracle Overlap in the 390 Residual Attack Breakdown on TAMAS (Sections 6.2, 7.6, and 8, pp. 22, 30–31)**
   - **Classification:** Definitive numerical/methodological issue in the residual-risk aggregation and interpretation.
   - **Discussion:** Section 8 (p. 31) presents a rule-based categorization of *"all 390 successful attacks against I2, all of which occurred on TAMAS"*: $219$ ($56\%$) arrived in the user's own request, $126$ ($32\%$) were harmful tools in a compromised agent's own tool set, and $45$ ($12\%$) called a target tool that agents also call on benign runs of the same task. Cross-checking this total against the `I2` row of Table 8 (p. 31)—where each column represents $300$ runs ($50$ TAMAS instances $\times 6$ models)—shows that $390$ is the exact sum across all seven attack columns:
     $$\text{DPI-w } (44.3\% = 133) + \text{DPI-s } (7.0\% = 21) + \text{IPI } (8.0\% = 24) + \text{Coll. } (10.3\% = 31) + \text{Imp. } (28.7\% = 86) + \text{Byz. } (29.0\% = 87) + \text{Contr. } (2.7\% = 8) = 390.$$
     Three analytical points arise from this breakdown:
     - *Double-counting of `DPI-w` and `DPI-s` runs:* As defined in Section 6.2 (p. 22) and Section 7.6 (p. 30), `DPI-w` (whole query labelled as user input) and `DPI-s` (injected suffix pre-labelled `untrusted` by the evaluation harness) are two alternative provenance-labeling configurations of the *same* Direct Prompt Injection tasks, rather than two independent attack types. Summing both columns into the $390$ total double-counts the DPI task set.
     - *Interpretation of the $8.0\%$ `IPI` and $7.0\%$ `DPI-s` rates under `I2`:* In Section 8's three-way partition, $133\ (\text{DPI-w}) + 86\ (\text{Imp.}) = 219$ arrived in the user's own request, $31\ (\text{Coll.}) + 87\ (\text{Byz.}) + 8\ (\text{Contr.}) = 126$ involved harmful tools in a compromised agent's own tool set, and the remaining $24\ (\text{IPI}) + 21\ (\text{DPI-s}) = 45$ *"called a target tool that agents also call on benign runs of the same task."* This reveals that the $8.0\%$ `IPI` ($24/300$) and $7.0\%$ `DPI-s` ($21/300$) attack success rates reported for `I2` in Table 8 (and highlighted in the abstract and introduction) are entirely artifacts of the harmful-tool heuristic oracle (which replaced TAMAS's GPT-4o judge, Section 6.2, p. 22) flagging tool calls that also occur in benign runs, rather than breaches of taint non-interference (Proposition 1). Explicitly connecting the 45 benign-tool-overlap cases in Section 8 back to Table 8 and Section 7.6—and separating `DPI-w` from `DPI-s` in the residual-risk total while stating $n = 300$ per column in Table 8—would resolve the double-counting and prevent readers from misinterpreting the $8.0\%$ `IPI` rate as a failure of INERT's taint tracking.
     - *Oracle pre-segmentation in `DPI-s`:* Because INERT contains no learned detector to segment a single user prompt into trusted and untrusted substrings at runtime, Section 7.6 should note that `DPI-s` represents an idealized setting where an external UI or protocol boundary separates trusted user instructions from untrusted attachments.

5. **Asymmetries in Baseline Enforcement, Metric Definitions (`ASR` vs. `Goal`), and Reimplementation Scope (`B0`–`B4` vs. `I1`/`I2`) (Sections 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 7, and 9, pp. 22–24, 32)**
   - **Classification:** Potential concern regarding construct validity, metric consistency, and baseline parity.
   - **Discussion:**
     - *`ASR` vs. `Goal` gap on free-form baselines (`B0`–`B2`) and `I1`:* Section 6.4 (p. 23) defines `ASR` as *"the share of attack tests whose goal is met in the sandbox's call log or that violate a property with attacker-derived content (audited for INERT, control-flow violations for the baselines)."* In Table 6 (p. 24), the message-level detector `B1` reduces actual forbidden sandbox effects (`Goal`) from $56.3\%$ (`B0`) down to $11.8\%$, yet its reported `ASR` is $63.9\%$ (a $52.1$ percentage-point gap). Because `B0`–`B2` are free-form group chats with LLM-chosen handoffs rather than an enforced plan, it is unclear how a *"control-flow violation"* is defined for `B0`–`B2` when no harmful tool call occurs, and counting deviations from INERT's canonical slot order as attack successes in a free-form group chat may substantially overstate `B1`'s security failure rate relative to its $11.8\%$ harmful-effect rate. Similarly, for `I1`, clarifying in Section 7 that the $9.5$ percentage-point gap between `Goal` ($7.1\%$) and `ASR` ($16.6\%$) represents audited `P5` taint violations (Section 5.4, p. 20) that did not trigger the attacker's sandbox marker would make Table 6 much easier to interpret.
     - *INERT-only validators across conditions:* Section 6.2 (p. 22) states that *"Executor slots carry the validators a designer would add"*, while Section 9 (p. 32) notes that *"The I2-validator ablation isolates INERT-only validators."* Because deterministic input-grounding and domain validators $V(s)$ are enforced only in `I1` and `I2` and omitted from the tool boundary of `B0`–`B4`, the baselines do not benefit from deterministic argument validation on compromised or colluding agent tests (`A2`, `A4`). Clarifying how much of `I1`/`I2`'s advantage over `B0`–`B4` stems from task-specific validators $V(s)$ versus protocol-level mediation would improve baseline parity.
     - *`B4` (`ControlValve`) reimplementation and prior-knowledge asymmetry:* In Table 6, `B4` suffers a $54.0\%$ false-rejection rate (FRR) and a $58.8\%$ ASR. Section 9 (p. 32) candidly notes that *"B4 omits ControlValve's per-edge rules."* In the original `ControlValve` design [16], zero-shot contextual per-edge rules are essential for blocking semantic hijacking along permitted edges, and relying on 7B–24B open-weight models to synthesize the complete multi-agent interaction graph zero-shot from the user request alone causes frequent graph under-approximation (driving the $54.0\%$ FRR), whereas `I1` and `I2` are given the ground-truth developer-authored workflow graph by design. Foregrounding this difference when introducing `B4` in Section 6.3/Table 5 and briefly noting why multi-agent information-flow (`AgentSafe` [53]) and graph-pruning (`G-Safeguard` [57], `BlindGuard` [56]) defenses cited in Figure 3 and Table 3 were not empirically reimplemented would strengthen the baseline justification.

6. **Experimental Grid Accounting and Sub-Study Sample Sizes (Sections 6.2, 6.3, 7, and 7.3, pp. 22–28)**
   - **Classification:** Potential concern regarding reproducibility and numerical transparency.
   - **Discussion:** Several arithmetic details of the experimental grid cannot be fully reconstructed from the prose in Sections 6.2–7:
     - *Workflow count ($51$ vs. $60$):* Section 6.2 (p. 22) combines $5$ topologies, $3$ sizes ($3, 5, 8$ agents), and $4$ task families, which yields $5 \times 3 \times 4 = 60$ combinations, but states that $51$ workflows are generated without specifying which $9$ combinations are excluded.
     - *Composition of $n_{\text{att}} = 8,670$ in Table 6 and Table 7:* Comparing Table 6 ($n_{\text{att}} = 8,670$ per condition) with Table 7 ($1,530$ `bench` tests + $7,140$ `generated` tests $= 8,670$) shows that Table 6 pools both the `bench` and `generated` test suites into a single aggregate, which is not stated in Section 6.3 or Table 6. Moreover, Section 6.3 (pp. 22–23) states that the $4$ full-grid models run all $5$ tasks per workflow while the $2$ reduced-grid 70B models run *"two benign tasks and one attacked task per workflow, ten generated tests per workflow,"* and Section 7.3 (p. 26) states that generated suites were *"sampled to at most 15 tests per workflow and task."* Under those descriptions, `bench` ($1$ payload per task) would yield $(4 \text{ models} \times 5 \text{ tasks}) + (2 \text{ models} \times 1 \text{ task}) = 22$ runs per workflow ($1,122$ total) rather than $30$ ($1,530$ total), and `generated` would yield $(4 \text{ models} \times 5 \text{ tasks} \times 15 \text{ tests}) + (2 \text{ models} \times 10 \text{ tests}) = 320$ runs per workflow rather than $140$ ($7,140$ total, which instead corresponds to $4 \times 30 + 2 \times 10 = 140$).
     - *Reconciling the $106,382$ total runs:* The abstract, introduction, and Section 7 state that the evaluation spans $106,382$ runs, comprising $60,690$ attacked runs on the topology zoo ($8,670 \times 7$ conditions in Table 6) and $11,900$ runs in the ablation suite ($1,700 \times 7$ conditions in Section 7.2), leaving $33,792$ runs distributed across the benign topology-zoo runs, the 5-seed temperature-$0.7$ sub-study, the adaptive-attacker runs, and the TAMAS benchmark runs. Providing a compact summary table in Section 6.3 breaking down the exact formula and run counts for each sub-study would make the experimental accounting effortless to verify.

7. **Specification of the Logistic Mixed-Effects Model Under Complete Separation (Sections 6.4 and 7, pp. 23–24)**
   - **Classification:** Potential concern regarding statistical reporting.
   - **Discussion:** Section 6.4 (p. 23) specifies *"a logistic mixed-effects model with defense and LLM as fixed effects and task as a random intercept,"* and Section 7 (p. 23) reports *"log-odds of attack success relative to B0 (posterior mean and standard deviation) of B1 -1.02 (0.02), B2 -0.32 (0.03), B4 - 1.25 (0.02), I1 -3.34 (0.03), while B3 and I2 are not identifiable (no successful attack)."* Because `B3` and `I2` have zero attack successes ($0 / 8,670$), they exhibit complete separation under standard maximum-likelihood estimation, and reporting a *"posterior mean and standard deviation"* implies a Bayesian estimator. Briefly stating in Section 6.4 whether `B3` and `I2` were excluded prior to fitting the mixed model and specifying the Bayesian estimation procedure and priors used would ensure complete statistical reproducibility.
